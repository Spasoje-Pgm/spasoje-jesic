Insurtech · Salesforce · UK · 24 months
Insurance MGA — Enterprise Delivery Transformation
10 teams 6 environments Multi-vendor C-level stakeholders

A UK-based MGA on Salesforce with 10 parallel teams, multiple vendors, and a delivery operation that had lost stakeholder confidence. I identified release governance as the highest-leverage entry point — fixed it completely, then used the credibility earned to address the full enterprise program. Scope grew from 4 to 10 teams not as a new assignment, but as a result of what the first work proved.

What I did

  • Restructured release planning into two-stage sessions — scope definition, then testing strategy, dependency mapping, and go-to-market
  • Defined QA exit criteria per environment — Dev → QA → Integration → SIT → UAT → Production
  • Introduced semantic versioning, runbooks, deployment windows, and entry/exit gates across all environments
  • Introduced Prod-1 as a dedicated rollback environment — separated recovery from the release pipeline entirely
  • Established Delivery Lead model across all 10 teams — decisions handled at team level, not escalated to program
  • Managed multi-vendor shared codebase with full dependency mapping and C-suite performance visibility
  • Mapped happy path to anchor automation — regression × 2 per env, sanity per team, sanity in production

Government · Africa · 6-month fixed deadline
Ministry of Sports — Multi-Platform Greenfield Delivery
5 teams 4 products Mobile + B2C + B2B + Admin

Four digital platforms built from nothing in six months for an international sporting event with a non-negotiable launch date. Zero legacy. Five parallel teams. The strategic question was not how to build — it was how to organize delivery so the event launched regardless of what went wrong.

What I did

  • Proposed microservice architecture as a delivery decision — decoupled products so teams could develop and release independently
  • Implemented MoSCoW prioritization at feature level before sprint one — gave stakeholders a governed framework for trade-off decisions
  • Organized delivery into 10 iterations with locked scope — prevented requirements shifting while teams were mid-build
  • Resolved design sign-off bottleneck through modularization — components approved independently rather than full-product reviews
  • Escalated infrastructure resources early and introduced deployment windows — protected team velocity while DevOps track caught up
  • Embedded ministry stakeholders in bi-weekly prototype reviews — UAT became a confirmation exercise, not a discovery exercise

Approach

How I work.

01
I look for the system failure, not the symptoms
My first two weeks on any engagement are not about fixing anything. They are about mapping the full program — delivery process, environment architecture, team workflow, dependency structure, stakeholder confidence, commercial performance. Fix the structure and symptoms resolve. Fix symptoms and the structure keeps producing new ones.
02
I start where the leverage is highest
Complex programs have more problems than any single intervention can address simultaneously. I find the constraint generating the most downstream damage and move on it first — completely. Half-solving the highest-leverage problem is worse than not solving it, because it creates the illusion of progress without removing the constraint.
03
I build systems, not workarounds
The difference between a delivery manager and a firefighter is what they leave behind. Every intervention I make is designed to be repeatable without me — runbooks any engineer can follow, gates any team can apply, planning sessions any delivery lead can run. When I leave a program it should run better than when I arrived.
04
I manage upward as deliberately as I manage delivery
Senior stakeholders lose confidence not because delivery is slow — they lose it because they cannot see what is happening. Uncertainty is more damaging than bad news. My reporting model eliminates uncertainty: weekly status with wins, losses, and risks named explicitly. No sanitizing. No hiding slippage behind optimistic language.
05
I treat people performance as a delivery variable
Programs fail because the people running them do not have clear goals or a clear picture of what success looks like for them personally. When I managed 15 project managers, the fastest lever for improvement was sitting with each PM individually — understanding their projects, giving direct feedback, defining their next development step.
06
What does not change across contexts
I have worked in insurtech, government, digital agency, fintech, healthtech, and renewable energy. The domains change. The method does not: understand the full system before touching any part of it. Find the highest-leverage entry point. Build repeatable structures. Communicate honestly with stakeholders at every stage.
